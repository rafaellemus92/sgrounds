A mood ring for typing

A classic mood ring reads the temperature of your finger. This one reads the temperature of your typing. Every keystroke is a pulse; the sphere turns the pulses into colour, and the rings carry the last few seconds of you outward like ripples on water. Type steadily and the colour deepens. Rush and it heats up. Stall and it cools back to violet.

The pitch is simple: you cannot not watch it. A continuous, honest picture of your own rhythm is the fastest feedback loop there is, and feedback is how you become a speed typer at breakneck speed.

The colour math

Colours are computed in OKLCH, a perceptually uniform colour space, so equal steps in your typing look like equal steps in colour. Three numbers drive it:

  • Hue ← velocity. Keys per second map onto a path from violet (still) through blue, green, gold and amber to red (breakneck). Cool is slow, hot is fast, like a thermometer.
  • Chroma ← sync. How saturated the colour is comes from how locked your rhythm is. Scattered keystrokes give a greyish, uncertain tone; a steady beat gives a vivid one.
  • Lightness ← accuracy. Mistakes dim the sphere. Idle dims it too; it sleeps when you do.
  • Rings ← time. Ring k shows the colour from k × 0.33 s ago. The innermost ring is a third of a second behind you; the outermost is a little over two seconds behind. Your past propagates outward, and the small wobble travelling through the rings is the oscillator's phase, delayed ring by ring.

The sync math

Inside the sphere runs one oscillator with phase φ and natural frequency ω. Each keystroke is a pulse that kicks it, the same pulse-coupled model Mirollo and Strogatz used for fireflies and Peskin used for the heart's pacemaker cells, and its frequency slowly adapts to your rate:

between keys     dφ/dt = ω
on a keystroke   φ ← φ − K·sin φ            K = 0.35
                 ω ← ω + ε·(2π·v − ω)       ε = 0.25,  v = 1 / interval

Just before each kick the oscillator's phase is recorded. If you type with a steady rhythm the kicks always land at the same phase and the oscillator locks to you. The sync number is the Kuramoto order parameter over the last twelve keystrokes:

r · e^{iψ} = (1/N) · Σ e^{iφ_j}

r = 1 means perfect lock: you and the ring are one clock. r near 0 means the kicks are scattered. Crickets, fireflies, heart cells, pendulum clocks on a shared beam and typists all do the same thing when they are coupled: they find a beat.

The wager

Typing is a two-oscillator system. One is in your head and produces the next letter; one is in your hands and executes it. The hypothesis this toy makes visible is that the velocity of thought shows up as phase coherence in the keystrokes, and that a continuous picture of that coherence, a colour you cannot help but watch, trains the two oscillators to lock faster. Breakneck speed is not more force. It is better coupling.

In the language of sgrounds the rings are wyrd: the thread already spun, radiating outward from the moment you are in. The semicolon holds the two oscillators together without collapsing either.
