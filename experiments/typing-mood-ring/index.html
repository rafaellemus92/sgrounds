    patterns

    A mood ring for typing

    A classic mood ring reads the temperature of your finger. This one reads the temperature of your typing. Every keystroke is a pulse; the sphere turns the pulses into colour, and the rings carry the last few seconds of you outward like ripples on water. Type steadily and the colour deepens. Rush and it heats up. Stall and it cools back to violet.

    The pitch is simple: you cannot not watch it. A continuous, honest picture of your own rhythm is the fastest feedback loop there is, and feedback is how you become a speed typer at breakneck speed.

    The colour math

    Colours are computed in OKLCH, a perceptually uniform colour space: a change in lightness or saturation looks as large as it measures, whatever the hue. Three numbers drive the colour of the sphere, and a fourth, time, drives the rings:

    • Hue ← velocity. Keys per second move along a thermometer from violet (still) through blue, green, gold and amber to red (breakneck). The scale is stretched through blue and green, where most people type, and compressed at the red end, so the colours line up with the mood words.
    • Chroma ← sync. Saturation is measured as a fraction of the most vivid colour the screen can show at that hue and lightness, so a steady beat reaches full colour at every speed. Scattered keystrokes stay greyish.
    • Lightness ← accuracy. Over your last twenty characters; in the free tab there is nothing to copy, so only speed and stillness move it. One slip dims the sphere a little, a run of them darkens it, and it brightens again as you type cleanly. Idle dims it too; it sleeps when you do.
    • Rings ← time. Ring k shows the colour from k × 0.33 s ago. The innermost ring is a third of a second behind you; the outermost a little over two seconds. Your past propagates outward, and the small wobble travelling through the rings is the oscillator's phase, delayed ring by ring.

    The free tab can keep what you write, if you press save, exactly as you typed it. Entries stay in this browser on this device; export is the backup, and nothing leaves the page. Despite the name, the colour and the mood words (still, calm, flowing, quick, blazing, breakneck) read only the mechanics of typing: its speed and rhythm, and accuracy in the tabs with text to copy.

    The sync math

    Inside the sphere runs one oscillator with phase φ and natural frequency ω. Each keystroke kicks it. It is a cousin of the pulse-coupled models Mirollo and Strogatz studied for fireflies and Peskin for the heart's pacemaker cells: a phase oscillator with a sinusoidal phase-response curve, entrained by your keystrokes, whose frequency slowly adapts to your rate.

    between keys     dφ/dt = ω
on a keystroke   φ ← φ − K·sin φ
                 ω ← ω + ε·(2π·f − ω)

K = 0.35   ε = 0.25   f = 1 / interval

    The phase is read at the moment of each keystroke, just before the kick. A pause longer than three beats (and at least 0.6 s) is a rest, not a beat: the oscillator waits for you instead of slowing down. After 2.5 s of silence the rhythm starts over. Held-down keys repeat, but repeats are not keystrokes.

    The sync number is the mean resultant length of those phases over your last twelve keystrokes, the same formula Kuramoto uses for his order parameter:

    r · e^{iψ} = (1/N) · Σ e^{iφ_j}

    r = 1 means perfect lock: you and the ring are one clock. r near 0 means the kicks are scattered. Crickets, fireflies, heart cells, pendulum clocks on a shared beam and typists all do the same thing when they are coupled: they find a beat.

    Sprint

    One word at a time, the way NextWord Sprint began. No spaces: the word clears itself when the last letter lands. The arc around the ring is your fuel, and its colour is the pace you must hold, starting at 1.5 keys per second and rising by 0.05 with every word. Sync multiplies each word, five clean words in a row raise the combo, and a gold rune from the loom is worth double. Your best run comes back as a ghost, and the line under the word counts how far ahead or behind it you are.

    Duel: Bifrost and Snell's law

    In the duel each typist is a medium for light. Your refractive index is your interval in breakneck units, n = interval / 83 ms: light slows in glass because each wavelength takes longer, and you are denser because each letter does. Between the two spheres stands Bifrost, the bridge that lets some light cross and turns the rest back.

    Snell        n₁ · sin θ₁ = n₂ · sin θ₂
critical     sin θc = v_slow / v_fast
through      T = min(1, v_you / v_them)²
push         dp/dt = g · (I_you·T_you − I_them·T_them)

    Where you are faster, all of your light crosses and bends toward the normal, converging on the other sphere. Where you are slower, rays steeper than the critical angle reflect totally and stay on your side. T is the fraction of a glowing sphere's light that escapes, sin²θc, which is the same factor as the n² brightness theorem. I is how bright your light is, (1 + sync)/2 times your recent accuracy, so coherence and care count as much as speed. The push p takes the other sphere's rings from the outside in; seven rings or forty-five seconds decides it. Both velocities get a floor of 0.5 keys/s, so two idle typists are a draw and nothing divides by zero.

    The opposite colour wheel. In the duel you own the warm half of the wheel, violet through red to gold as you speed up, and your opponent owns the cool half, green through cyan to blue. At equal speeds your colours are exact complements, 180° apart. Light that crosses Bifrost takes rings in its own colour.

    Loki. Light that turns back is not lost. It charges Loki, and when the meter fills, for two seconds your light ignores the critical angle and theirs cannot reach you: the branch nobody saw coming. Only the side that is losing light charges it.

    Opponents. A jötunn is a giant who types at a steady pace with a human wobble and gets four words a minute faster each time you beat it. Urðr is the keystroke timing of your own last duel, replayed against you: what was, against what is.

    An honest note. Typing speed is a rate, not the speed of light, so the duel is a picture and not a law of typing. The law that does govern two typists is the oscillator one. Two coupled oscillators whose frequencies differ by Δω lock when |Δω| ≤ 2K, at a phase lag where sin ψ = Δω / 2K, the same sine-equals-ratio shape as Snell's law. When |Δω| > 2K there is no locked solution and the phases slip past each other: that is its total internal reflection. Snell's law itself is proved in Lean in this repository, as snell in wyrd-mathlib/WyrdMathlib/Rainbow/Defs.lean.

    The wager

    Typing is a two-oscillator system. One is in your head and produces the next letter; one is in your hands and executes it. The hypothesis this toy makes visible is that the velocity of thought shows up as phase coherence in the keystrokes, and that a continuous picture of that coherence, a colour you cannot help but watch, trains the two oscillators to lock faster. Breakneck speed is not more force. It is better coupling.

    In the language of sgrounds the rings are wyrd: the thread already spun, radiating outward from the moment you are in. The semicolon holds the two oscillators together without collapsing either.

    Rhythm Lab

    exploratory prototype · tapping to a beat · no claims about mood, empathy, intelligence, health or typing

    Does a phase-locking model describe how you tap along with a steady beat? The lab measures your own comfortable pace, then plays beats a little faster or slower and records when you tap. Sound only; the screen stays still.

    Before you start

    On this device only, the lab records the time of each click it plays and each tap you make, which input you used (touch, mouse or key), the beat settings, the date and time of each session and trial, and basic timing details of the device: the audio delay values and sample rate your browser reports, its clock resolution, and whether the screen is a touch screen. It does not record typing, journal text, your name or anything about health. Nothing is uploaded. Export makes a file you choose to save, and reset deletes everything.

    Your taps against the beat

    Each dot is a tap, plotted as how far it sits from its beat in cycles, without re-centring on the nearest beat. A flat line is a steady offset; a slope is drift; crossing a whole cycle is a slip. Each tap counts toward the beat nearest where you have been tapping. Missed beats are skipped without counting as slips; a second tap for the same beat is an extra tap, and the one nearer your running pace is kept; duplicate events within 80 ms are ignored. The taps column counts the taps used.

    simulate dδ/dt = Δ − k sin δ

    δ is your phase minus the beat's. Δ is the detuning, how much faster your own pace is than the beat, and k is a coupling rate. Both are in radians per second. Stable locking happens when |Δ| < k; |Δ| = k is the boundary. The plot shows −δ/2π, where a tap would sit, with the same sign as your tap plot: up is after the beat.

    k is not loudness, and nothing here assumes people obey this equation: the lab checks whether it describes your taps.

    how timing works, and its limits

    Every time is read from one clock, the browser's performance.now(). Clicks are scheduled ahead on the audio clock, and each click's output time is converted to that clock with the audio system's own timestamp. Taps use the event's timestamp on the same clock.

    The page cannot see Bluetooth delay (often 100–300 ms), and touch screens add about 20–80 ms before a tap is timestamped. A constant delay is fitted as one offset and never read as anticipation; changing delay adds noise. A trial is flagged and not counted if the page is hidden, loses focus, the audio is interrupted or stalls, or the audio clock mapping moves by more than 10 ms during the trial.
