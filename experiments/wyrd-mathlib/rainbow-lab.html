Rainbow Ray Lab

A Lean formalization of classical rainbow geometry

Sunlight enters a raindrop, bends, reflects once or twice inside, and leaves. Lean proves which ray turns least. Rays bunch up near that ray, which is why the bow appears there, though brightness is not modelled. From that ray follow the order of the colours and a gap between the bows in the rays reflected once or twice. Move the sliders to see each result. Each result below carries one of these labels:

The drop

One ray, traced through the drop

  1. Move Entry angle i. However you move it, the viewing angle of a once-reflected ray never passes about 42°. That ceiling is the primary bow.
  2. Press Go to the bow ray, then choose 2 · secondary and press it again. The secondary bow is about 51°, and no ray reflected once or twice lands in between.
  3. Press Red, then Violet. The bows move in opposite directions, so the colours swap order between the two bows.
  4. Press Show where the angles differ. There the signed angle that the theorems bound is −49°, which the theorems allow, yet the ray is seen at 49°, inside the 43°–50° band.

Solid lines are the signed angles the theorems bound: π − D after one reflection and D − π after two. The model behind the proofs.

Lean takes the turning angle D(i) = kπ + 2i − 2(k+1)·arcsin(sin i / n) as a definition. It assumes ray optics in one plane through a perfect sphere, a point sun, one real index per colour with no absorption, and entry angles from 0° to 90°. The indices 1.331 (red), 4/3 (water) and 1.343 (violet) are inputs. The theorems bound the signed angles π − D and D − π. Viewing.lean (22 October 2026) proves that π − D is the viewing angle of every once-reflected ray exactly when n ≤ √2, and that D − π is the viewing angle of every twice-reflected ray when 1.331 ≤ n < 2. Numerically the secondary identity holds down to n ≈ 1.17996; that stretch is not proved.

Note on prior work.
The mathematics is classical: Descartes (1637) and Newton (Opticks, 1704). A limited search, as of 22 October 2026, found no earlier machine-checked proof of these rainbow results, to our knowledge.
  • We searched Lean and Mathlib, Physlib and large Lean archives, Isabelle, HOL Light, HOL4, Rocq, Mizar, Metamath, ACL2, PVS and Agda, and the published literature.
  • The closest formal work is the HOL Light optics library of Siddique, Aravantinos and Tahar, which treats refraction only in the small-angle approximation. In Lean, FormalPhysics derives Snell's law at a flat surface.
  • GitHub code search was unavailable to us, and some papers could only be read as abstracts.
